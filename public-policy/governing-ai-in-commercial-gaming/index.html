Governing AI in Commercial Gaming

A risk-based supervisory approach to player protection

Anubhav Khanna 4 August 2026 Executive MPA (LSE) · PMP · Certified AI Generalist

Read the full brief (PDF) Discussion on LinkedIn

The problem

Artificial intelligence now mediates decisions that used to sit with people: which promotion a customer receives, when a session is interrupted, whether someone is flagged as at risk. The rules governing those decisions, by contrast, were largely written for static systems configured by hand.

The same technical capability can push in either direction. It can intensify harm by optimising engagement among the most susceptible customers, or reduce it by detecting markers of harm earlier and more precisely than manual review allows. Which outcome prevails is a question of supervisory design, not technological inevitability.

The asymmetry that matters

A model that identifies a customer whose play indicates escalating loss-chasing produces a single output that is simultaneously a protection trigger and a commercial opportunity. The same signal supports an intervention and a reactivation offer with equal technical ease.

Nothing in the technology determines which one follows, and nothing in an operator’s commercial incentives reliably favours the first. That is precisely why the choice cannot be left to internal governance, and precisely where a supervisor should intervene.

A four-tier framework

The brief proposes classifying operator AI by how close it sits to a decision affecting a customer, and whether the objective it optimises runs with or against that customer’s interest.

Prohibited. AI-selected inducements, bonuses, credit offers and reactivation prompts directed at customers displaying defined markers of harm, at those who have set protective limits, and at the self-excluded.

High risk. Customer-facing decisioning that determines promotional treatment, personalised pricing, loyalty tiering or content sequencing. Pre-deployment assessment, a documented statement of the objective function, meaningful human oversight, decision logging sufficient to reconstruct an individual case, and monitoring against customer outcomes rather than commercial measures alone.

Protective systems. Harm detection and intervention triggers, mandatory above a licensee size threshold — with validation evidence for the model’s performance in the licensee’s own population, and a positive duty to act on the flags it generates.

Minimal. Back-office monitoring, fraud and verification systems, already governed elsewhere. Inventory disclosure only.

Supporting instruments: an AI-systems inventory as a licence condition, incident reporting, accountability for vendor-supplied models, a supervised pilot route for protective tools, and hybrid policy-and-technical capability inside the Authority itself.

Where the argument is weakest

Four objections are treated as serious rather than answered away.

“AI” is not a stable regulatory category, so the trigger is defined by function rather than by technique. Personalisation is not inherently manipulative, and a broad ban on personalised marketing would be both over-broad and, in a market competing for operators, unsustainable — the prohibition has to be narrow to be defensible.

Harm-detection models are adequate rather than strong, and mandating them risks mandating instruments of unestablished validity — which is why validation evidence, and not possession of a system, has to be the compliance test.

And supervisory capacity is the binding constraint. Every obligation above presupposes an Authority able to interrogate a model, not merely a policy document about a model. If that capability is not built, the framework produces compliance theatre and a false assurance worse than the acknowledged gap it replaces.

Why now

The UAE has established a regulator and a licensing framework in advance of a substantial domestic market. Most jurisdictions regulate retrospectively, layering obligations onto operators whose commercial systems were designed without reference to them, and pay for it through enforcement.

The argument for acting early is not that the risk is acute today. It is that the cost of specification rises steeply once systems are built and revenue depends on them.

Scope, disclosure and limits

This is a discussion paper written in a personal capacity. It represents no institutional position and constitutes neither legal advice nor a compliance assessment. It argues from regulatory design and published evidence, not from primary research, and makes no claim to have measured the prevalence or effect of AI-mediated promotion in any market. Regulatory instruments cited were current as at July 2026.

The empirical literature it draws on comes overwhelmingly from remote online markets, where account-based play generates continuous individual-level data. That data-richness assumption holds strongly for carded and online play and weakly for anonymous cash play, and the transferability of specific findings should not be overstated.
